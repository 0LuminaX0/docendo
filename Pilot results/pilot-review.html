Recommendation

  • Keep one question per idea, the two why-questions included. The greedy question drew out the only real misconception in the pilots, A's “starts to exploit each day their option which had the highest score from the first test”, and Kai would fail P7 on it. That is recursive feedback at its best.
  • Drop the follow-ups that name a missing fact, such as “What happens to an option's bonus each time it gets picked?”, and ask “anything else?” once.
  • Check three keys in code, the way “ever more slowly” is checked today: lock-in needs “never … again”, “stuck” or “never corrected”; UCB's rule needs “highest” or “max”; a pick needs a direction (“smaller”, “lower”, “shrinks”). Tried on eleven pilot quotes, the three credits above become partial and the six clear answers still pass. Two edge cases to decide: a UCB formula without “pick the highest” (L) and a “highest” in the sentence after the quote (P).
  • Keep the planted misreading as the fallback for a learner whose notes cover everything. On these three notebooks it would no longer come into play.

P's fuller version, one broad question per rule, should go through eval:sim before a person meets it. Its risks are fewer misconceptions said out loud, and problems with several gaps that one correction can't fix. In L's first run a right correction left a second fact missing, and Kai stayed wrong.

Each extra error costs about 4 minutes of practice: a wrong first try with its correction took 5 to 6 minutes, a right one 1 to 2½.

Question 3

Are five test questions enough?

Seven make each score steadier, but no test length rescues significance with 20 learners. At 10 per condition even a perfect test finds the original effect about one time in three.

Power: the share of simulated studies in which a two-sided Welch t-test at 5% finds the difference. Ranges run from weaker to better items (discrimination and spread of difficulty), 3,000 simulated studies a cell, so ±1 to 2 points. The M4 plan's 36% is the analytic value for a perfect test.

  • Reliability. Seven items raise Cronbach's α in the simulation from .47–.65 to .54–.72, so one lucky guess moves a score less. That helps the item analysis and the per-learner measures more than the t-test.
  • The reasons are the cheaper gain. Scored 0 to 2 against the rubrics you already have and added to the choices, they gain more power than two extra items.
  • The pretest as covariate adds a little. If it correlates .3 with the test, the comparison is as precise as with 10% more learners; at .5, a third more.
  • Each learner recruited adds more than any of these. With about seven people asked so far (M4), every extra one counts.

One setting for two questions

A test item takes about 2 minutes, and most of it goes into deciding and writing the reason. Reading the setting takes perhaps 20 to 30 seconds. Two questions on one setting would save about one item's worth in 15 minutes, and give two answers that fail together when the setting is misread. Keep a setting per item.

The same number in both tests

Equal lengths are fine but buy nothing in the analysis. The pretest asks the reasoning the lesson builds on and names no rule; the test asks the rules. A gain from one to the other, as the M4 plan reports, compares two different tests. Use the pretest as the covariate and report the test score on its own.

  3. About 10 times as wide

A 95% confidence interval for an average, based on 25 readings, is 2.0 wide. About how many readings would make it 0.5 wide, if the readings vary as much as before?

Small samples land at both ends

r1

A country ranks its 1,000 schools by this year's average exam score. Schools have between 20 and 2,000 students.   2. Mostly small schools
  3. Schools of all sizes, in proportion to how common they are
  4. Mostly medium-sized schools
  5. I don't know
h3 (chosen)

Restaurant A has an average of 4.8 stars from 5 reviews; restaurant B has 4.6 stars from 500 reviews. Which restaurant's true average is more likely to be above 4.9, and which is more likely to be below 4.5?

Picking the best result picks up luck

r2

A company tries 20 new suppliers with one delivery each. The suppliers truly differ in quality, and each delivery's quality also varies by chance. The company gives a second order to the supplier whose first delivery was best. What should it expect from that second delivery?

  1. As good as the first delivery
  2. Better than the first delivery
  3. Worse than the first, still above the average supplier
  4. Worse than the first, and below the average supplier
  5. I don't know
h4 (chosen, reworded 13 Nov)

Shops X and Y each try out the same 20 suppliers, then give a big order to the one that did best. X tried each supplier once, Y ten times. Whose big order will likely fall further below how its supplier did in the trial?

  1. Y's
  2. About the same for both
  3. Neither: both will match the trial
  4. X's
  5. I don't know

h4: r2's key (“worse than the first, still above the average supplier”) is the cautious middle option, which a guesser picks without the idea; h4's two likely answers are opposites, X's or Y's. Reworded with shops and “did best”; the same options and key.

You only learn about what you choose

r3

A bank grants loans only to applicants its scoring rule rates as safe, and records whether each loan is repaid. Can the bank find out from these records whether its rule turns away many people who would have repaid?

  1. No, not from these records alone
  2. Yes, from these records alone
  3. Yes, once it has enough records
  4. Yes, if it also records the rejected applicants' incomes
  5. I don't know
h5

A company hires only applicants who score above 70 on an entry test. Among the people it hired, the test score barely predicts how well they do the job. Can the company conclude that the test is useless for choosing staff?

  1. Yes: among the people hired, the score barely predicts performance
  2. Yes, provided it has data on many employees
  3. No: everyone hired scored above 70, so the data hide what the score does
  4. No: it should first check that the test was marked correctly
  5. I don't know
r3b (chosen, new 13 Nov)

A bank lends only to applicants its scoring rule rates as safe, and records whether each loan is repaid. So far 97% have been repaid. Does the rule turn away many people who would have repaid? What do the bank's records show?

  1. That it turns away few of them
  2. That it turns away many of them
  3. Nothing, however many loans it records
  4. Nothing yet; more years of records will show it
  5. I don't know

r3b: r3's key was the only “No” among three “Yes” options, and the question nearly gave it away; h5 gives a reason in every option. r3b keeps the bank: two readings of the 97% (few, many) and two answers on whether more records help, none with a reason.

q1, q2, then k1, k2, h3, h4 (reworded), r3b (new), as built on 13 Nov: one set for everyone, so no assignment and no twin effects. O took about 5 minutes for items like these, so 7 minutes under a hard clock. Keep the screening questions first and uncounted, on the same page.

Learning gain. Don't add items to match the test's seven: with percentages the count doesn't matter, and two more prerequisite items wouldn't make the pretest measure what the test measures. Report pretest % and test % side by side, with one sentence saying the pretest measures the prerequisites. Run the comparison of conditions on the test with the pretest as a covariate. What was measured

  • Pretest: about 45 seconds a question (O: seven in 5:10, five in 4:06). Two screening questions and seven items take about 6½ minutes.
  • Reading: about 9½ minutes for the three chapters, O and A alike (A's 19:31 includes 10 minutes away from the tab).

  • Test: about 2 minutes an item on the first form (O 13:02 for five, with revisions; L 8:52), quicker on a second.

Soft or strict

Keep soft timing, as in the pilot. Reading and teaching end on their own (the text closes, Kai runs out of questions), and a hard stop on the pretest or the test turns slow answers into wrong ones: under the first run's limits L lost 3 pretest and 4 test answers. If the M4's strict limits stay, give the pretest 8 and the test 17 minutes as a margin for the slowest.

A fourth practice problem or an eighth test item would each add about 2 minutes.

How the checks were made

  • Kai's practice: each notebook was rebuilt from the verdicts and wrong beliefs on its session page, then gate() and caveatFor() decided Kai's first try on P7, P3 and P2. Gate open counts as right, as in every eval:sim run with the gate open (34 of 34). The stricter-keys column turns the three credits without the key into partial; the no-follow-ups column drops the answers given to follow-ups.
  • Key checks: candidate patterns for lock-in, UCB's rule and a pick shrinking its bonus, tried on eleven quotes from the pilots.
  • Power: simulated learners whose ability the condition shifts by d; items answered through a logistic model with 15 to 20% guessing and difficulties near the middle; reasons scored 0 to 2 from the same ability with their own noise; Welch's t-test, two-sided, 5%.
